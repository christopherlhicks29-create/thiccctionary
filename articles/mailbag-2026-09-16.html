Filed Replies, Vol. 16

Bartholomew Whitmore, Senior Cataloguer

From T. Okafor, Portland:

I submitted a pumpkin last spring. You rejected it. I just saw you accepted an Atlantic Giant in 2009. What's the difference?

My pumpkin was conventionally farmed and weighed 847 pounds. The Atlantic Giant was 1,872 pounds and selectively bred. I think you owe me an explanation.

The 2009 Atlantic Giant Decision (Precedent 2009-03, available at /about/documents/atlantic-giant-2009/) extended eligibility under Section II.4 to cultivated and selectively bred subjects, but it did not lower the bar, it defined the floor. Your 847-pound conventionally farmed pumpkin cleared neither the Silhouette Test under Amendment 1991-08 nor the contextual-mass threshold that has governed gourd submissions since the original Style Guide. Filed a formal explanation to the submissions desk, where it will sit alongside the other forty-three letters from people who believe girth is a matter of personal opinion. It is not.

From D. Szymanski, Tucson:

My neighbor's horse is, by any reasonable measure, extremely thiccc. I submitted it in July. No response. Are large animals even eligible?

Large animals are eligible; your submission form, however, arrived with the Silhouette Test panel left blank, which is not a technicality but the entire methodology, as established by Amendment 1991-08 at /about/documents/amendment-1991-08/ and repeated, in plain language, in Section III of the Style Guide. I have a file cabinet in this office that contains nothing but incomplete large-mammal submissions, and it is the heaviest object on this floor, heavier than whatever is in that cabinet is the weight of my professional regret. Resubmit with the panel completed. Include a lateral photograph. Do not submit a photograph taken from above; it tells me nothing useful and suggests a certain optimism about the animal's dimensions that the catalogue cannot reward on faith alone.

From P. Blanchard, Montreal:

The coffee situation. I heard you've been raising this since 2018. It is now 2026. Have you considered simply buying a new machine yourself?

The coffee machine in the editorial break room is institutional property, which means its repair falls under Constance Pribyl's operational mandate, a fact I established in Grievance No. 14 (2018), reaffirmed in Grievance Nos. 22 and 31, 38, 41, 44, and 47, all of which are a matter of record in the Personnel File at /about/documents/personnel-file/. Constance has, at each interval, indicated that the matter is "parked for Q3 review," a phrase she has now used across portions of four separate fiscal administrations. I do not purchase institutional property out of pocket. I also do not litigate the same point eight times without result, and yet here we are, 7:54 in the morning, September 16, 2026, and I am drinking something that came out of a gas station on Route 9 because Teddy Whitmore-Adjacent thought the break room Keurig he brought from home "counted." It does not count.

From R. Fairweather, Edinburgh:

Spider Hennessy filed a dispatch last month datelined Nairobi. The postmark on the physical copy we received here at our archive was Lagos. How do you account for this?

Spider Hennessy's expense reports have been postmarked from a city other than the one named in the dispatch for as long as Spider Hennessy has been a correspondent for this publication, which is longer than I can verify because his original contract is in a box that Margie Whitmore has had aboard the Margaret IV since at least 2021 in connection with a filing she describes as "ongoing." The arrangement with Spider functions because his work is accurate, timely, and submitted in the correct format, which is more than I can say for several people who occupy desks inside this building and receive a W-2. Filed a note to the records desk. The note joins the others.

From O. Vennström, Stockholm:

The United States seems to be in a prolonged institutional crisis. Do you have any thoughts?

The diagnosis is correct but the framing is imprecise. What you are observing is a submission backlog combined with an unresolved classification dispute and a Style Guide that key stakeholders have chosen to treat as advisory rather than binding. Several branches of the government have been resubmitting the same brief under different cover letters since roughly 2016, each time with a new title and the same structural deficiencies the previous submission contained. The Founding Charter equivalent, their Constitution, is a clear document with an established Appendix process, and the problem is not that it lacks amendment procedures but that the parties in disagreement have each decided their preferred reading supersedes the plain text of Section I. We resolved a dispute of comparable severity in 1991, and it required a formal amendment, two external reviewers, and one very long Tuesday. It produced Amendment 1991-08, which still holds. I am not saying the Americans should adopt the Silhouette Test. I am saying they need a methodology and someone willing to enforce it, and that person should not also be submitting entries for review.
